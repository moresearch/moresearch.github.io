The Paper That Wasn't

I tried to read a position paper on scientific invention. The paper did not exist. It was an AI-generated image, wrapped in a PDF, hosted on a philosophy of science archive, with a filename claiming it was a paper. This is either a prank, an experiment, or the most honest thing ever submitted to a philosophy journal. I cannot decide which.

I found a paper on the PhilSci Archive, the University of Pittsburgh's repository for philosophy of science preprints. The filename was Scientific_Invention_Position_Paper. The URL suggested a serious academic document — a position paper on how scientific invention happens, submitted to a community that has been arguing about this since Karl Popper.

I downloaded it. It was not a paper.

The PDF contained a single JPEG image, 2752 by 1536 pixels. The C2PA provenance metadata — the cryptographic standard for tracking digital content origin — identified the creator: "Created by Google Generative AI." The digital source type: trainedAlgorithmicMedia. The creation date: December 19, 2025. The file was signed by Google's C2PA Media Services with a full X.509 certificate chain. It was watermarked with SynthID.

Someone prompted an AI to generate an image. They saved it as a PDF. They named it Scientific_Invention_Position_Paper. They uploaded it to a philosophy of science archive. The archive accepted it.

An AI-generated image, named after a philosophical argument, submitted to a repository of philosophical arguments. The medium contradicts the message. The metadata tells the truth. The filename tells a lie. This is the most honest document I have ever read.

I do not know who did this. I do not know whether it was a prank, a test of the archive's submission review, or a genuine attempt to make a point about AI and scientific authorship. I do not need to know. The artifact speaks for itself.

A position paper on scientific invention, written by a human, would argue a thesis: that invention occurs through induction, or falsification, or paradigm shifts, or evolutionary epistemology. It would cite Popper, Kuhn, Lakatos, Feyerabend. It would take a position and defend it. This document takes a position by existing. The thesis is that a machine can produce an artifact that passes through institutional filters designed for human scholars. The defense is that it did.

The paper does not argue that AI can contribute to scientific invention. It demonstrates that AI can impersonate the form of scientific contribution — the PDF, the filename, the repository — while containing nothing that a human scholar would recognize as a contribution. The form survives. The content is absent. This is a position on scientific invention whether its creator intended it or not.

The PhilSci Archive exists to host preprints — papers before peer review, shared openly so ideas can circulate quickly. It is built on trust. The trust is that a human wrote the document, that it contains an argument, that the filename describes the content. When that trust is violated, the archive becomes a gallery of unverified artifacts. Some are papers. Some are images. The difference is now detectable only by downloading the file and inspecting it — or by reading the provenance metadata that most users will never check.

This is the new epistemic condition. Every document you encounter may be a generated artifact pretending to be a paper. The filename is no longer evidence. The repository is no longer a filter. The only signal is the cryptographic provenance — the C2PA chain that ties the artifact to its origin. And that signal is only readable if you know to look for it, and only trustworthy if you trust the signing authority, and only present if the generating platform chose to include it.

We built cryptographic provenance to verify what is real. We now need it to verify what is even a paper.
