I just bought a new keyboard: a Corne 4x6. It is the first board I have owned where the layout, the firmware, and the way I type are all going to change at the same time. I have been circling split ergonomic keyboards for a while, and this felt like the right moment to stop reading about them and actually commit.

![Corne 4x6 — 42 keys, split, column-staggered](images/corne-4x6.jpg)

## The board

The Corne — originally the crkbd — is a 42-key split keyboard: three rows of six columns per side, plus three thumb keys per half. The "4x6" name comes from the switch matrix on each half: three six-key rows plus a fourth row for the three thumb keys. It is column-staggered rather than a flat grid, so each finger gets its own column at its own height, and the thumbs finally get real keys instead of being relegated to the space bar.

Mine is a non-metal build. I wanted a metal case, and that is still the version of this board I have in my head: aluminum gives the keyboard enough weight to stay put on the desk, changes the sound from something hollow to a low, solid thock, and makes it look like a tool rather than a toy.

But I could not find one. So I settled for a non-metal case for now. The long-term plan is to make my own custom metal case one day, once I have the time and the workshop access to do it properly.

## Why a metal Corne was hard to find in Brazil

This was not a casual purchase. Finding a metal Corne in Brazil was genuinely difficult.

The local mechanical keyboard scene is small, and the niche-within-a-niche of split ergonomic boards is smaller still. Most Corne kits are sold as group buys or small batches from abroad, and metal cases are usually the most limited part of the run. Add international shipping, import taxes, and customs clearance, and the effective price of a nice aluminum case can double before it reaches your desk. Local sellers who do stock split boards tend to carry acrylic or 3D-printed cases, because those are cheaper to produce and easier to ship.

So the search meant watching classifieds, importers, and group-buy leftovers for months, comparing case materials, and deciding whether to settle for acrylic or pay the import premium. In the end, I did not find a metal case I was willing to pay for, and I was not going to let the search keep me from actually using the keyboard. I bought the non-metal build, and I am treating the custom metal case as a separate project for later.

That is the honest trade-off: I get to start learning the layout now, and the board I really want is still ahead of me.

## The plan: Miryoku, Colemak-DH, matrix

Now the hard part begins. I am going to learn three things together:

1. **A split matrix layout** — six columns per hand, column-staggered, with thumbs doing real work.
2. **Colemak-DH** — the layout on the keys.
3. **Miryoku** — the layer system that makes 42 keys act like a full-size keyboard.

That is a lot of change at once, and I know it. But the three pieces are designed for each other.

### Colemak-DH

[Colemak-DH](https://colemakmods.github.io/mod-dh/) is a variant of Colemak that keeps the familiar QWERTY positions for `Z`, `X`, `C`, and `V`, so common shortcuts such as `Ctrl+C` and `Ctrl+V` still work with the same hand shape. It moves `D` and `H` onto the home row for better rolls and less lateral stretching, and it keeps most punctuation within easy reach. Compared with QWERTY, it is less about raw speed and more about comfort: fewer awkward same-finger bigrams and fewer long reaches.

The catch is that the first two weeks feel like typing with someone else's hands. I expect my speed to fall off a cliff before it comes back.

### Miryoku

[Miryoku](https://github.com/manna-harbour/miryoku) is Manna Harbour's minimal layout philosophy for small split keyboards. It organizes the board into layers:

- **Base** for letters.
- **Nav** for arrows, Home/End, Page Up/Down, and window management.
- **Symbols** for brackets, punctuation, and programming characters.
- **Numbers** for a numpad-style cluster.
- **Function** and **media** layers for the rest.
- **Mouse** keys, so the hands can stay on the keyboard for small pointer movements.

Miryoku is built around the 36-key form factor (three rows of five columns plus three thumbs per hand), but it adapts to a 4x6 Corne: the extra outer columns can host modifiers, macros, or stay unused, and the top row gives a bit more room for numbers or function keys. The important idea is not the exact key count. It is that every layer is reachable from the home position, with home-row mods — `Ctrl`, `Alt`, `Shift`, and `GUI` on the home keys when held — so the pinkies stop stretching for modifiers.

### Matrix

The Corne is a matrix in the sense that each half is a grid of switches wired in rows and columns. Typing on it is not the same as typing on a staggered row board: the columns are vertical and staggered by finger length, so the fingers move mostly up and down instead of diagonally. For someone raised on a standard keyboard, the first few days are disorienting. After that, the geometry starts to make sense — especially for the pinkies and the thumbs, which no longer have to reach sideways for common keys.

## What I expect to get out of it

I am trying to be honest about the benefits, because a new keyboard is easy to romanticize.

**Less finger travel.** On a 40% split board, almost everything is one layer away from the home row. Numbers, symbols, navigation, and function keys stop requiring a trip to the edges of a 104-key rectangle. Over a long writing or coding session, that adds up.

**Modifiers without pinky gymnastics.** Home-row mods put `Ctrl`, `Alt`, `Shift`, and `GUI` under keys my fingers already rest on. Instead of curling a pinky to the bottom corner for every shortcut, the modifier is a held home key. This is the change I am most curious about, and also the one I expect to mis-trigger most often at first.

**Thumbs that do something useful.** The three thumb keys per side can take `Space`, `Backspace`, `Enter`, and layer shifts. Thumbs are strong and underused on a normal keyboard; giving them real work should reduce strain on the other fingers.

**A more symmetric posture.** Splitting the board lets my shoulders sit at roughly their natural width, and the column stagger keeps my wrists straighter. I am not expecting the keyboard to fix everything — sleep, breaks, and desk height still matter — but the geometry should stop fighting me.

**Navigation and mouse on the keyboard.** The Nav and Mouse layers mean I can move the cursor, select text, switch windows, and scroll without moving my right hand to a mouse. For editing and code review, that is a quiet but constant win.

**A layout I chose instead of inherited.** QWERTY is a historical accident. Colemak-DH is not magic, and it will not make me a faster typist by itself, but it is designed around the way fingers actually move. Learning it deliberately also forces me to pay attention to habits I had stopped noticing.

## The costs

The costs are real too:

- **Speed will drop.** Probably from around my normal typing speed to something embarrassing for a couple of weeks.
- **Mistakes will be frequent.** Wrong layer, wrong modifier, accidental `Ctrl` chord. Home-row mods are notorious for this until the timing is tuned.
- **Two layouts in my head.** I still need QWERTY on laptops and other people's machines, so I will keep a QWERTY layer or a toggle available.
- **Configuration time.** Firmware, keymaps, tapping terms, and layer tweaks are their own hobby. I am trying to timebox this rather than endlessly rebuilding.

## How I plan to learn it

I am going to take the transition in stages instead of changing everything on day one:

1. **Type on the Corne in QWERTY first** for a few days, just to get used to the split geometry and the thumb keys.
2. **Switch the alpha layer to Colemak-DH** and do daily drills until the home row stops feeling foreign.
3. **Add Miryoku layers one at a time**, starting with numbers and symbols, then navigation, then mouse keys.
4. **Track speed and accuracy weekly**, not daily. Daily numbers during a layout change are noise.
5. **Keep a QWERTY fallback** on the laptop so real work does not grind to a halt.

I will write a follow-up once I have a few weeks of data: typing speed, accuracy, which layers clicked, and whether the non-metal build held up. Eventually there should be a second follow-up about the custom metal case, too. For now, the board is on the desk, the keymap is flashing, and the first drill is waiting.
