Adviser at the Top, Caseworker at the Desk: The UAE's Two Answers for AI in Government

Two Gulf News stories, two days apart, show where the UAE is putting AI in government. At the top: the Cabinet AI Adviser System — 32 specialised AI assistants that analyse policies and legislation, benchmark international best practice and follow up on decisions around the clock. At the desk: Dubai Land Department's Initial Registration platform, where AI reads Emirates IDs and sales contracts, fills in applications and approves eligible transactions on submission. One is an adviser that never decides. The other is a caseworker that decides the routine cases. That division of labour is the real answer to the question of AI's role in government.

In the UAE, September opens the new government season, and the first Cabinet meeting of the season opened with a new member that is not human. Gulf News reported that Sheikh Mohammed bin Rashid Al Maktoum chaired the meeting at Qasr Al Watan in Abu Dhabi and approved the launch of the Cabinet AI Adviser System: 32 specialised AI assistants that analyse policies, legislation, strategies and government systems.

The system is not a search box with a government skin. It assesses the potential impact of proposals, reviews international best practices and provides recommendations — then stays on the job, working with ministers around the clock to follow up on decisions and their implementation. Sheikh Mohammed framed the goal in one line:

"Our goal is a more efficient government, with faster and more accurate decisions."

Read that sentence as a product spec. Faster: AI compresses the time between a policy question and a policy answer. More accurate: it raises the information density of every answer — impact analysis, comparative practice, implementation status. The adviser exists to make the staff work of governing cheaper and faster, not to make the authority cheaper.

Two days later, a quieter announcement showed the same doctrine at a different altitude. Gulf News reported that the Dubai Land Department (DLD) launched the "Initial Registration" platform, which folds project registration, real estate transaction registration and escrow account management into a single digital process. Developers stop re-entering data and resubmitting documents; AI reads Emirates IDs, passports and sales contracts, extracts the relevant information and populates application fields automatically. Transactions that satisfy the department's business rules can be approved on submission, without a human in the loop for the routine case.

"By deploying artificial intelligence and strengthening integration among stakeholders, we are enhancing the ease of doing business and reinforcing transparency and governance," said Abdullah Ahmed Al Shehi, CEO of DLD's Real Estate Regulatory Agency.

Add a "Project 360" view that consolidates unit status, escrow accounts and financial data with early-warning indicators, and the shape of the second role appears: an AI caseworker that handles intake, validation and the straight-through processing of eligible transactions, while humans hold the exceptions and the oversight.

Two placements, one question: what role should AI play in government?

The two stories are two different answers, and the distinction matters more than the technology in either announcement.

Role one: adviser, never decider. In the Cabinet, the AI does what a human cabinet office does — prepare dossiers, benchmark against other jurisdictions, model consequences, track execution — but continuously and at the scale of 32 parallel specialisations. The deliberate design choice is that it advises. Ministers decide, ministers answer. Accountability is not delegated; it is sharpened, because the person deciding now has better information and fewer excuses. The most telling detail is the closing of the loop: the assistants "follow up on decisions and their implementation." That is not question-answering; it is an agentic workflow over the decision lifecycle, converting a one-off meeting outcome into a monitored, traceable object.

Role two: caseworker, with gates. In the Land Department, AI does the document intake, the cross-system data reuse, and the approvals that the business rules already permit. Nobody is claiming the AI is a regulator. The platform reduces the proportion of applications that bounce back for minor omissions, cuts manual entry, and surfaces early-warning indicators for intervention — automation that raises the quality of the human work upstream and downstream of it.

Both roles sit under one doctrine. The DLD story notes the launch follows Sheikh Mohammed's framework targeting half of UAE government sectors, services and operations to run through autonomous, self-executing agentic AI models within two years. The Cabinet meeting's own numbers supply the substrate: more than 308 million digital transactions through 1,633 digital services in a year, 92 per cent satisfaction with priority services, and first-place global rankings in telecommunications infrastructure, digital skills and AI adoption. The digital services layer was the precondition; the AI layer is the current step. You can only add an adviser to a government whose decisions, documents and follow-ups already exist as data.

What I take from this pair of announcements:

  1. The role question precedes the technology question. Whether AI in government acts as adviser, caseworker or decider is an organisation-design choice, not a model choice. The UAE is staffing the first two roles explicitly and keeping the third vacant — so far.
  2. Placement is the strategy. Put AI where staff work and routine work live, not where accountability lives. The Cabinet adviser is surrounded by accountable humans; the registration caseworker is surrounded by explicit business rules. Both placements scale capacity without relocating responsibility.
  3. The governance engineering is still ahead of the announcements. Cabinet-level recommendations need audit trails, documented disagreement, and a defined relationship between an assistant's recommendation and a minister's stated reasons. Straight-through approvals need appeal paths and error budgets. "Assess the impact of policies" is a mandate that will eventually have to include the impact of the assessor's own failure modes.
  4. Sequencing is the part other governments will copy. This follows the playbook from the earlier UAE run: train the humans first — 80,000 federal employees, a judiciary trained to oversee agentic systems — then put AI in the services, then in the staff offices, and only then at the Cabinet table.

Governments will not be replaced by AI. They will be reorganised around two AI roles: an adviser that compresses the time and cost of deciding well, and a caseworker that clears the routine so humans can spend their attention on the exceptions. The UAE is the clearest demonstration yet of both roles running at once — adviser at the top, caseworker at the desk, decider still human.
